<h4>last seen</h4>

<p>season three of the wire. wow. wow!!!!!!!!!! an absolutely exhilarating ride, with some of the most incredible scenes of this show so far. i sat down to start to write this and kept remembering new things i’d forgotten, which only goes to show that everything feels vital and necessary in this show.</p>

<p>this is bunny colvin’s season, in many ways. a man of courage, conviction and who is just plain damn tired. a few months out from retirement from the force, after years of seeing his district collapse, he proposes a radical idea: legalising drugs in a few areas of his part of town that are abandoned and derelict. dealing can go on in these “free zones”, but nowhere else in the district.</p>

<p>i think what this season shows really comprehensively is the distance between the politicians and the streets. bunny’s idea might have been on his mind anyway, but would he have got the courage to do it had he not witnessed other majors being relentlessly bullied in comstat meetings for having rising crime figures? that pressure, we learn, is directly due to councillors and the mayor demanding that crime figures go down ahead of election season. the response from other districts? all the things you do when you’ve been told you’re at war, and that you cannot lose: fudging the figures, funnelling more men into the battle.</p>

<p>disagreeing with this approach, bunny decides to do something different: trying to save, as he puts it, what’s left of his district. i really enjoyed watching the creation of “hamsterdam” play out. there’s the initial issue of the dealers not wanting to move; then the discovery of an old woman still living in a row of houses bunny thought was abandoned; then the fact that they have no customers and people have to be rounded up off the streets and deposited in the free zones. at this point, i still thought of it as a broadly good idea.</p>

<p>but things are never that simple on the wire. in a truly harrowing scene, after everything has been up and running for a while, bubs walks through hamsterdam at night, and through his eyes (and mind you, bubs has seen some shit) we begin to understand the horrors taking place there; the damage that heroin and an addiction to it can wreak. there’s also the issue of the kids, which carver in particular feels strongly about. they used to be “employed” by the hoppers and dealers as police look-outs, part of the elaborate schemes that kept the exchange of drugs for money opaque. now, they’re running amok, bearing witness to some truly traumatic scenes.</p>

<p>it’s clear that making drugs legal (in a certain area, that is) isn’t a simple fix-it. there also need to be social programmes: help for kids, for people who are addicts, for sex workers. needle exchanges, hiv testing. these do get introduced by bunny’s church friend, and his contacts—but since all of this is a secret from the district commander and the mayor, they can only go so far. the wire is so good at these moments: the grey areas, the in between. is it perfect? no. was it always doomed to fail? yes, but that doesn’t mean it wasn’t worth trying. the final scene of bunny standing in the razed free zone, and bubs, unaware that he’s talking to the architect of hamsterdam, cheerfully describing how great the freezones were…. absolutely fantastic.</p>

<p>and there are hints that what bunny created will continue on, that it is still worthwhile to strive for something good, and in particular that we cannot give up on those kids: specifically in the form of dennis. dennis, formerly a soldier in the game, is released from prison at the beginning of the series. he tries his hand at honest work, doesn’t like it, ends up back on the streets with avon’s crews. but the escalating violence as avon starts warring with marlo’s crew finally, decisively, puts an end to dennis’s career in the game. instead he gets involved with bunny’s church friend, and decides he wants to do something for the kids. he’s a boxer, so why not a gym in one of the free zones? </p>

<p>initially, it’s slow going—there’s a fantastic scene showing him being bounced around different administrative people all of whom tell him he needs x thing and to get y permission and to do z. a little bit of wheedling by a reverend who donates a lot to certain city councillors, and things go a bit smoother for dennis. he finds it hard to get kids to come along, but is given a piece of advice by another boxing gym owner: you can’t give up on them. you have to show them that you are going to be there for them no matter what. so dennis does, and by the end he has a motley crew of little fighters. carv walking into it in the final scenes made me so happy!!!</p> 

<p>to talk more about carver: i loved him this season. possibly the only other police officer who really understood and cared about what bunny was trying to do. he really tries to stay loyal to bunny’s vision for the free zones, but also displays a deep affinity and care for the kids!! he gets together money from the dealers to buy a basketball hoop for them, referees their games. “this is my corner for kids who can’t play nice”. there’s also a fantastic scene between him and bunny where bunny expresses his disappointment at how carver doesn’t know his streets, doesn’t know the population that lives in his district. carver says nothing, but he takes the message: later, there’s a community meeting in the district, and a woman talks about how finally, for the first time in years, a local police officer introduced himself to her.</p> 

<p>meanwhile, stringer trying to corrall his wayward group of gangsters into an orderly, polite business operation starts off funny, as he forces his dealers to request the floor during a meeting (“chair don’t recognise your ass!” / “well, poot did have the floor”), but quickly sours once avon is released from prison. stringer’s desire to resolve things bloodlessly, through negotiation, is rejected by avon, who tells him, “i guess i’m just an old-fashioned gangster. i want my corners.” </p>

<p>frustrated, stringer leaves him to it, focusing instead on his “legitimate” hustle: getting involved in property development. there’s a kind of schaudenfreude to watching the smooth-talking, slippery stringer grapple with the fact that he’s completely out of his depth here. but it’s hard to get any real satisfaction out of seeing him be played so comprehensively by the odious senator clay davis, who walks away with hundreds of thousands of dollars of drug money, and nobody the wiser. stringer, for all his ambition, his belief that he can use one world to get his foot into another, is realising that it might not be so easy as all that.</p>

<p>the balance of power between stringer and avon is a key theme of this season, and there are some absolutely killer scenes between them. i thought the central conflict and eventual split would come from avon finding out that stringer arranged for dee’s death. but after many attempts of trying to stop brianna finding out that his death probably wasn’t a suicide—stringer actually tells avon himself. they have a brief, intense, brutal fight, which stringer wins (avon is injured, but still). at this point, the scales seem tipped in stringer’s favour: he’s the one who manipulates, who organises, whose paranoia has kept the cops off their back for two years. avon is his “attack dog”, as described by prop joe: unruly, uncontrolled.</p>

<p>but that attack dog might just be getting stringer into hot water: it’s his cavalier dropping of bodies that gets daniels, mcnulty and co back on their gang, and mcnulty and kima in particular are desperate to get stringer, after he evaded capture two years before. and stringer’s also under pressure from prop joe and the other top dogs in the city, who threaten to remove stringer and avon from the alliance that gets everyone a share of the good dope, if stringer can’t call off avon. stringer tries, but eventually he has to admit defeat: he can’t get avon to stop. and so he calls in an anonymous tip to bunny colvin, hoping to send avon straight back to jail. </p>

<p>but avon isn’t done learning about the ways that stringer has betrayed him: brother mouzone is back in town!!! after learning that omar only shot him because stringer claimed he was responsible for killing omar’s boyfriend, brother mouzone goes directly to avon. and avon’s not dumb: he sees exactly why stringer wanted to get rid of brother mouzone.</p> 

<p>from there, we get one final scene of the b&b brothers: on the rooftop, swapping stories of their wayward youth. we know stringer’s turned avon in to the police; but what we don’t yet know is that--maybe out of revenge for dee, or maybe he’s just sick of stringer manouevering behind his back—avon has turned over stringer to brother mouzone and omar. yet another example of the widening gulf between them: stringer works through the authorities, but avon turns to street justice. </p>

<p>i have never felt crazier than when stringer heads into his property development and the camera pans to omar and brother mouzone, stepping out into the street. absolutely INSANE moment, and it only gets better. omar walking through the door to the fluttering of bird wings…… wow. wow wow wow!!!!!!! of course, it’s curtains for stringer—and once again, he evades our friends on the wire, who just hours before had been celebrating that they’d finally caught stringer talking about illegal activity on record. avon isn’t so lucky though—it’s back to prison for you my friend!!! </p>

<p>we have to talk more about omar. a fantastic season for him. he’s still at his stick-up game, which turns violent when he can’t let go of his grudge against avon’s people: his friend and ally tosha dies in a brutal shootout. bunk is the officer on the case, and witnesses some kids re-enacting the scene, clamouring to be the one to act as omar. the ensuing scene between omar and bunk is one of the best in the season. bunk and omar come from the same part of town, and bunk, trembling with fury, tells omar about how people used to look after each other back when he was growing up. how he’d be itching for a fight and instead of teaching him a lesson, the hard boys would just tell him to go home. think about the kids, he tells omar, think about those kids watching you and what they’re growing up to believe about the world. omar says nothing, spitting on the ground when bunk leaves, but he takes it to heart. it sits on his conscience, and he does something for bunk: pays out of his pocket to get a stolen police service weapon back. he’s a man with a code, and honour, which is more than you can say for a LOT of these characters. and he finally gets to take his revenge on his old enemy, stringer bell. chef’s fucking kiss. </p>

<p>there’s always humour in the wire: bodie’s fury at being stopped and searched when he was on the way to hamsterdam; brother mouzone telling his henchman, “your homophobia is so visceral” right before sending him into all the gay bars of baltimore; rawls patronising one of said gay bars; the sunday truce debacle (“people are saying we shot omar’s granny in the ass!”), bernard and his long-suffering girlfriend traipising up and down the state to get burner phones. </p>

<p>again, the lack of female character development is blatant. ronnie will never get a plot that doesn’t involve a man; kima is rapidly becoming as deadbeat a dad as mcnulty; brianna only gets a look in when we need someone to feel sad and angry about dee. and yet, even despite the latter, there is one stunning scene between brianna and mcnulty, where they discuss the fact that dee can’t have killed himself. mcnulty takes her to task a little for preventing dee from flipping—an act that we now know probably led directly to his death. brianna, emotional, asks: “why go to donette? why not come to me?” mcnulty, in perhaps his best moment in the whole show thus far, says, “i guess i was looking for someone who cared about the kid. you were the one who convinced him to take the years, weren’t you?” CHILLS.</p> 

<p>i simply must shout out the cinematography and the blocking of some of these scenes. brianna and avon in the foreground, with stringer at the back: fantastic. the close-up shots of bunk and omar during their confrontation: stunning. kima and mcnulty standing over stringer’s body: delectable. daniels opening the door on rawls, burrell and co: gorgeous. omar and brother mouzone at either ends of the alley during their intense, quiet, charged face-off: i am putting it directly in my mouth and gnawing on it like a dog with a chew toy. impeccable, deliberate lighting and staging choices, all adding to the feeling and the impression you’re meant to take from the scene. 10 thousand stars.</p>

<p>other things i didn’t have time to mention: marlo sat in the back of avon’s pre-trial hearing.... the cycle continues…. that slimy worm carcetti being dragged by bunny…. when he walks through hamsterdam and is completely harrowed because for all his posturing about crime he doesn’t understand the actual reality of the streets…. “prez would have loved this”…… d’agostino and mcnulty’s awkward as fuck dinner and him finally giving her the boot....</p>

<p>everyone says season 4 is the traumatic one. wish me luck</p>

<h4>reading</h4>

<p>i read <i>cassandra at the wedding</i> by dorothy baker last month, and woof. WOOF. every single person who picks up their pen to write a new Miserable Girl novel needs to first read this book and ask themselves whether what they have to say is more profound or as cuttingly, searingly delivered as cassandra at the wedding. the premise is very simple: cassandra is driving home to her family ranch rural california to attend her twin sister judith’s wedding. cassandra and judith had previously lived together while studying at berkeley, apparently both wanting a shared future together, even going as far as to buy a second hand bösendorfer grand piano for their apartment. very soon afterwards, judith leaves for new york, and has returned months later with a fiancé in tow, leaving cassandra devastated. cassandra is going home, and she’s determined to stop the wedding at all costs.</p>

<p>this book contains some of my favourite things: lucid, sharp descriptive writing; the careful outlining of feelings that seem impossible to put into words; vivid, alive characterisation; moments that are so shocking and intense that you want to get up and walk around just to expel the way it makes you feel.</p>

<quote>I tossed her the towel and had the feeling I get when I look at her, or when I look very self-consciously into a mirror, a feeling of being pulled apart and put together.</quote>

<p>i don’t want to spoil the events of the book, because i think it’s best experienced with no preconceptions, but i will say this. cassandra wants to belong at all costs, and she thought she had a forever home in judith. she is very, very lonely. this makes her sympathetic, and we do feel for her, but at the same time her love for judith manifests in wanting to control judith, to possess her—it honestly verges on psychosexual at some points. she is angry that judith could leave her, but she is also hurt that judith could do something as quotidian as get married, when she has such big plans for them, the two of them, together. she can’t love anyone but judith, and that suffocates judith, drowns her in it. and in the end she has to leave cassandra, to free them both from the strangling vines of cassandra’s love—to go and find her own place to belong. and maybe cassandra will too.</p>
